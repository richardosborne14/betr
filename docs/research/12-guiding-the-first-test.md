# Guiding the first test — what to say, when, and how small to make it

**Researched 2026-09-08**, after the founder watched their eldest daughter walk the new build
screen. Written for the founder and Misha. Claims are labelled **FOUND** (source read),
**INFERRED** (reasoning) or **UNKNOWN**, as in [`10-cbt-gateway-approach.md`](10-cbt-gateway-approach.md),
and every FOUND claim carries a URL. Nothing here is adapted wording from CCI, Getselfhelp,
Therapist Aid, Psychology Tools or the Beck Institute (rule 8) — their **methods** are read and
described; not one of their sentences is reused.

---

## 0. What was watched, in the founder's words

Three moments, and each is a different failure:

1. She wrote **"If I'm always blaming myself for mistakes, I'll not feel good."** It has the
   shape and none of the content: the *If* is a state she is in rather than a thing she'd do,
   and the *then* is a feeling only she can see. A person explained; she then wrote **"If I
   criticise my best friend, she'll not talk to me for the day"** — which is a clean,
   testable conditional assumption and exactly what BETR is for.
2. On **What will you do today?** she had no idea what was expected. The person explained again,
   and offered *"I'll give my best friend one small criticism tomorrow."*
3. She read it and said **"OH NO I can't actually give her a criticism, she'll not talk to me
   for the day"** — and left.

**Moment 3 is not a bug in her. It is the belief, at full strength, doing exactly what a belief
rated 10 does.** What a therapist supplies at that moment is a smaller step, out loud, with the
reason attached. BETR supplies four words: *"Small and entirely up to you."*

---

## 1. Verdict

1. **The missing thing is the rationale, and giving it is a measured intervention, not a
   courtesy.** Presenting a CBT rationale to people afraid of social situations raised their
   confidence about doing exposures and predicted **how many they actually did a month later**.
   **FOUND** §2
2. **Between-session tasks are among the strongest predictors of CBT outcome, and the
   literature puts responsibility for whether they happen on the therapist.** BETR has no
   therapist. Whatever that person would have done has to be on the screen or it does not
   happen at all. **FOUND** §2
3. **"Start small" is a real, published method with a real, published shape, and BETR has the
   slogan without the shape.** CCI's stepladder module names the dials a person turns to make
   one test easier or harder — who, how many, how well you know them, what, where, when, how
   long — and says plainly that the point is to find *a step you are willing to start with*.
   **FOUND** §3
4. **But small is not the goal, and this is the correction to the obvious fix.** Craske's
   inhibitory-learning work says the step should *maximally violate* the expectation, and that
   the aim is not to feel less anxious during it. A step shrunk until nothing could surprise
   her teaches nothing. The right size is the smallest one that could still turn out wrong.
   **FOUND** §4
5. **Her terror is itself a finding with a name.** People reliably over-predict how frightened
   they will be. And the asymmetry matters for design: over-predictions come down slowly, over
   many disconfirmations, while **a single worse-than-expected episode drives the next
   prediction sharply up**. That is the argument against a heroic first step, and it is a
   stronger argument than "be gentle". **FOUND** §4
6. **A worked example is the intervention for a novice, and BETR's worked example stops one
   beat before the step she got stuck on.** `content/examples.js` shows the prediction and then
   what happened. It never shows what the person *did*. **FOUND** §5, and it is in our own repo.
7. **None of this needs the app to decide anything.** Everything proposed is fixed content a
   person opens. That keeps it inside MHRA's "chapter in a book" gate and inside rule 2. The
   moment guidance is shown *because of* what somebody typed, it is the app choosing. §6

**INFERRED.** The founder's *Present · Practice · Produce* was right about the sentence and

leaving the safety behaviour out is the whole point, and why the first step is meant to be
small. Her father supplied all four in about ninety seconds. That is the content that is
missing, and it is fixed prose, which is the only kind BETR is allowed.

## 3. "Start small" has a published shape, and we have the slogan without it

Read in full: CCI, *Stepping Out of Social Anxiety*, Module 4, **Behavioural Experiment
Stepladders**. **FOUND**
https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/-/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Stepping-out-of-Social-Anxiety/Stepping-out-of-Social-Anxiety---Module-4---Behavioural-Experiment-Stepladders.pdf
It is a consumer module — CCI hands this to lay people with no clinician, which is why it is
the closest published thing to BETR's own position. **Its wording is restricted (rule 8); what
follows is its method, described.**

Five things it does that BETR does not:

1. **It names the dials.** A test is made harder or easier by changing *who is there, how many
   people, how familiar they are, how familiar the place is, what you are doing, when, where,*
   and how long for. That is the concrete answer to "make it smaller" — you do not water the
   test down, you turn one dial.
2. **It sets the bar at willingness, not at size.** It says outright that there is no right or
   wrong order, that starting with a high-anxiety experiment is fine if you are willing, and
   that the main thing is to find a step you are willing to start with.
3. **It tells you to expect anxiety** during the step, as a normal part, not a sign to stop.
4. **It says to repeat one before moving on** — because doing something once lets you put it
   down to luck.
5. **It has a named move for exactly her moment**: when a step has been tried and is still too
   distressing, build a *bridging* step between where you are and where it was.

It also says to make an appointment with yourself — day, time and place — which is the same
finding as Gollwitzer's d = 0.65 arriving from the clinical side. **BETR's `do` box asks for
none of the three.** Her sentence, as offered, was "tomorrow"; there was no where and no when.

## 4. Two corrections, so we do not overshoot

**4.1 Smaller is not automatically better. FOUND.** Craske et al.'s inhibitory-learning account
of exposure says the useful mechanism is **expectancy violation** — the gap between what you
expect and what happens — and that the first strategy is to design exposures that *maximally*
violate expectancies about how often or how badly the feared thing happens. It explicitly does
not treat within-session anxiety reduction as the index of success, and does not require a
hierarchy worked bottom to top.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864005/ ·
https://i-cbt.org.ua/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Craske-et-al-2014_-Maximizing-exposure-therapy_-an-inhibitory-learning-approach.pdf

**INFERRED, and this is the sentence that should govern the copy:** a step so small that no
outcome could contradict the prediction is not a small test, it is not a test. BETR's existing
suggestion already carries the right idea — *"in the smallest version that still counts"* — and
the load-bearing words are **still counts**. Whatever gets written must keep them and must
explain them. "Start small" alone is the wrong lesson.

**4.2 Her fear is over-prediction, and the asymmetry is the design constraint. FOUND.**
Rachman's review: many people overestimate how frightened they will be, and this is not
confined to people with a diagnosis. *Behaviour Research and Therapy* 32(7), 683–690, 1994.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7980354/
The follow-on finding in that literature is the one that matters for a tool with nobody in it:
**large reductions in over-prediction happen only after many episodes of disconfirmation, while
one episode that turns out worse than predicted drives the next prediction dramatically up.**
**FOUND** (match–mismatch model, Rachman 1994, and its exposure-therapy tests)
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0005796794900299 ·
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12197478/

**INFERRED:** a first test that goes badly costs more than a first test that goes small.
Together with §4.1 the rule is narrow and sayable: **the right first step is the smallest one
that could still turn out wrong.** That single sentence is the whole of the guidance BETR
needs, and everything else is worked examples of it.

## 5. The worked example stops one beat too early

**FOUND, in our own repo.** Every entry in `web/content/examples.js` has four fields:
`prediction`, `happened`, `from`, `to`. The front screen therefore reads: here is what somebody
was sure would happen, and here is what happened. **The doing is invisible.** A newcomer is
shown a prediction and an outcome and left to infer that a step existed in between — which is
precisely the inference she could not make.

The instructional-psychology finding is well established and points the same way: for people
who do not yet know how to do the thing, **studying a worked example beats attempting the
problem** — a medium-to-large advantage, g = 0.48 across 55 studies and 181 effect sizes, in
mathematics. Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla, *Educational Psychology Review*, 2023.
**FOUND** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1 ·
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1
Background and the original effect: Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham, *Review of Educational
Research*, 2000. **FOUND**
https://assess.ucr.edu/sites/default/files/2019-02/atkinsonderryrenklwortham_2000.pdf

**INFERRED, and the transfer is not free:** that literature is about algebra problems, not
about frightening things, so it argues for *showing the step* and says nothing about whether it
reduces fear. It also carries the **expertise-reversal effect** — worked examples stop helping
and start getting in the way once somebody knows how. That is the argument for every piece of
guidance below being a door somebody opens rather than a screen everybody walks through: the
founder is the expert case and the daughter is the novice case, in the same family, on the same
app.

## 6. What BETR may do, and the line it must not cross

The regulatory position is unchanged and it decides the shape of the fix, not just its wording.

- **Allowed, and comfortably.** Fixed prose that anybody can open and everybody sees identically
  is, in MHRA's terms, processing a user instruction to show fixed content in the manner of a
  reader choosing a chapter in a digital book. That is inside the low-functionality gate.
  ([`10-cbt-gateway-approach.md`](10-cbt-gateway-approach.md) §5.2, and it is where `why.js`
  already sits.)
- **Not allowed, and it is a short step away.** Guidance that appears *because of* what a person
  typed, a rating that decides whether they are shown the "make it smaller" page, a
  recommendation of which step to take, or anything that scores how frightened they are. That is
  the app choosing, it is rule 2, and it is the difference between a book and a device.
- **INFERRED, and it should be written into the task:** the rule for this whole piece of work is
  *the person opens the door*. No screen may appear on its own, in response to anything.

One consequence worth stating plainly, because it is counter-intuitive: **the app may not offer
her a smaller step, but it may teach her how to make one.** The dials in §3 are general
knowledge. Applying them to her friend is her doing, and it has to stay her doing.

## 7. What is already written and cannot be reached

**FOUND, in our own repo, and this is the cheapest finding in the file.**
`web/content/why.js` holds twenty-one entries of exactly the explanation this session is about
— what the worry actually is underneath, and *why leaving the safety behaviour out is the point*.
It is the best writing in the app.

It is unreachable for her, twice over:

1. It is keyed by a **stock worry id**. She typed her own sentence, which is the front door
   since B30, and an own test has no id in `BETR_WHY`. `whyLink()` renders nothing.
2. It appears **after a result**. The moment it would have helped is before the doing, which is
   the moment she left.

**INFERRED:** whatever gets built, the explanation has to be reachable from the two screens
where a person is writing and about to act, and it has to work for somebody whose test BETR has
never seen. That is one general "how this works" screen, not twenty-one more entries.

And one more, found while reading the code: **an unlocked draft is deliberately discarded.**
`go()` in `app.js` parks the loop on the way out, and the comment says so — *"an unlocked draft
is let go"*, a deliberate B8 decision so that nothing a person promised themselves is quietly
replaced. Her sentence — the one it took two people ten minutes to arrive at — was gone when she
closed it. That was the right call for a *promise*; it may be the wrong call for a *sentence*.
It is a founder decision, not a bug, and it is listed as one in the task.
